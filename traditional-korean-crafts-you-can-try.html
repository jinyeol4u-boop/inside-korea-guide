Traditional Korean Crafts You Can Try as a Visitor

By Dragon Kim · Updated October 31, 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer

The easiest place to try Korean crafts is Bukchon in Seoul, where the craft experience center runs 46 workshops with 16 local studios. Add the free Seoul Museum of Craft Art for context, or travel to Jeonju for hanji paper and Icheon for pottery. Most fees are not published, so confirm and book ahead.

What should I know before I go?

Details checked October 31, 2026

Treat craft workshops as bookable activities, not walk-in sights, and expect prices to be set by each studio. Seoul's city government lists the Bukchon center's reservations on its public booking platform, while smaller studios and provincial centers often ask you to phone ahead.

  • Worth knowing: Hanji (traditional Korean paper), maedeup (decorative knotting), mother-of-pearl work and ceramics are the crafts most often offered to visitors.
  • A common mistake: Arriving without a reservation. Programs can change and the Jeonju Traditional Hanji Center lists a minimum of 10 participants, so call first.
  • Local tip: Pair a workshop with a visit to the free Seoul Museum of Craft Art so you can see finished masterpieces before you make your own.

Which traditional crafts can visitors try?

Visitors can usually try hanji paper crafts, silk knotting, mother-of-pearl decoration and pottery. A May 2026 Korea Times report on the Bukchon center names knotted silk ornaments, mother-of-pearl crafts, coasters with temple-eave patterns, traditional weaving, ceramic repair and key rings shaped like a gat (a horsehair hat) among its offerings.

  • Hanji: Handmade mulberry paper used for lamps, boxes, frames and dolls. The Jeonju Traditional Hanji Center lists hanji doll, pressed-flower hanji and natural-dyeing programs.
  • Maedeup (knotting): Cords tied into bracelets, keychains and tassels, a popular first craft because the result is small enough for a suitcase.
  • Mother-of-pearl: Shell inlay on lacquerware, which the Bukchon programs also feature.
  • Pottery: Wheel or hand-built ceramics, with sessions running from a few hours upward, as described by iVisitKorea.

Practical example: with one free afternoon in Seoul, choose a short knotting or key-ring workshop in Bukchon, since it needs no travel outside the city.

How do I book a workshop?

Book through the city platform or phone the venue, and do it before your trip if you travel on a weekend or holiday.

  1. Pick a craft. Knotting and key rings suit short slots; pottery needs more time.
  2. Find the booking channel. Bukchon workshops are reserved through Seoul's public service reservation site; elsewhere, use the phone numbers listed above or the 1330 Korea Travel Hotline via VisitKorea.
  3. Ask three things. Price, how long it lasts and whether English support is available, since the sources do not state language options.
  4. Check the closing day. The museum and Jeonju hanji center close on Mondays, and Icheon's village closes on the last Monday of the month.

FAQ

Can foreign visitors join traditional craft workshops in Seoul?

Yes in practice, but check each program. The Bukchon Traditional Craft Experience Center takes bookings through the city's yeyak.seoul.go.kr platform, though the sources I read do not state language support, so confirm before you go.

Is the Seoul Museum of Craft Art free?

Admission is free according to Visit Seoul. It opens 10:00 to 18:00 (last entry 17:30) and is closed on Mondays; check the museum's site for current evening hours.

Where can I try making hanji paper crafts?

The Jeonju Traditional Hanji Center offers hanji doll, pressed-flower hanji and natural-dyeing programs for a separate fee, and Bukchon workshops also include paper crafts. Call ahead, because programs can change.

Is Icheon worth a trip for pottery?

If you want to make or buy ceramics, yes: the Icheon Ceramics Village has about 50 workshops, though it is closed on the last Monday of each month and opening hours vary by studio.
