Korean Cafe Culture Explained: Etiquette and Study Cafes

By Dragon Kim · Updated November 6, 2026 · 7 min read

Quick answer

Korea has more than 100,000 coffee shops, and the cafe is a social and study space as well as a drink stop. Expect a hot Americano from about 1,700 won at budget chains and 4,100 to 4,700 won at mainstream cafes. Sit as long as you like, but reorder if you stay for hours.

What should I know before I arrive?

Prices and rules checked November 6, 2026

Treat a Korean cafe as a place to stay, not just a stop, and expect rules about seats, outlets and cups to differ from shop to shop. Chains such as Starbucks, Twosome Place and Hollys sit beside small independent shops, and the two can follow quite different house rules.

  • Worth knowing: Many people spend hours in one cafe, which is why owners and chains now post stay and equipment rules. Starbucks Korea banned personal desktops, printers, partitions and multi-plug power strips on August 7, 2025.
  • A common mistake: Leaving your bag and laptop on a table for a long break. Starbucks Korea has said it worries about loss and theft when seats are left unattended, so take valuables with you.
  • Local tip: Learn two slang orders: a-a (iced Americano) and ttu-a (hot Americano). Staff understand a plain English "iced Americano" just as well.

How big is cafe culture in Korea?

Very big: Statistics Korea counted 100,729 coffee shops nationwide in late 2022, roughly double the number of convenience stores. The figure, reported by Korea JoongAng Daily, was up 4.5% on the year before and about double the 51,551 shops recorded in 2016. More than 84% of them are small operations with four or fewer staff.

Practical example: in a busy neighbourhood you can walk past several cafes in one block, so it is easy to skip a crowded one and pick a quieter shop around the corner.

How much does a cafe drink cost?

A hot Americano runs from about 1,700 won at a low-cost chain to roughly 4,100 to 4,700 won at mainstream cafes. Specialty, view and themed cafes can charge more, so read the menu board before you sit.

Can I work or study in a Korean cafe?

Yes, many Koreans do, but be considerate: buy something, keep your setup small and move on if the cafe is full. People who study or work alone in cafes are called cagongjok, a blend of cafe, gongbu (study) and jok (tribe). The Korea JoongAng Daily reports the word first appeared in print on April 21, 2015.

  • It is common: A 2021 Hankook Research survey of 1,000 people found 29% had done solitary tasks in cafes in the past year, including 60% of people in their 20s, as reported by the Korea Herald.
  • Owners count minutes: A Korea Food Industry Research Institute estimate cited in 2026 puts the break-even stay for an eight-table cafe selling a 4,100 won Americano at about 1 hour 31 minutes.
  • Rules vary: Some cafes cap stays, cover outlets or ask for a second order, and Ediya posted a sign at one store asking guests who stay over three hours to order more, according to the Korea Times.
  • Good manners: A professor quoted by the Korea Herald says using a cafe to work is acceptable when you think of other customers and match your stay to what you buy.

Practical example: if you plan two hours of remote work, order a drink on arrival and a second drink or snack around the one-and-a-half-hour mark, and use a laptop only, with no extra screen or printer.

What is a study cafe, and should visitors use one?

A study cafe is a paid, quiet study space, often unmanned, and it is mainly built for residents rather than tourists. It is different from a normal cafe: you pay for access by the day or month, not for a drink. The Korea Times reported in April 2026 that private study cafes charge monthly fees of 150,000 to 200,000 won, while public study spaces in Seoul's Mapo District cost 5,000 won a day for adults.

The Seoul city government warned in a consumer survey that 79 of 341 study cafes it checked (23.2%) had "no refund" clauses, so read the terms before you pay. For a visitor, a regular cafe is the simpler choice when you just need a seat, a drink and Wi-Fi.

How do I order and sit down like a local?

Order and pay first at the counter or kiosk, pick up your drink when it is called, then choose a seat. Most cafes work this way; a few table-service places differ, so watch what other guests do.

  1. Look for the ordering point. Use the counter or a kiosk, and switch the screen to English if there is a language button.
  2. Say the size and temperature. Ask for "iced" or "hot", and say whether it is for here or takeaway.
  3. Pay. Cards are widely accepted; see our guide to cash or card in Korea.
  4. Wait for your name, number or buzzer. Pick up the drink at the pickup counter yourself.
  5. Clear up when you leave. Some cafes have a return shelf for mugs and trays, so look for one before you go.

On cups: paper cups have been allowed inside shops, but the enforcement of in-store single-use rules has been on a grace period. In December 2025 the ministry announced a plan to end free disposable cups, with a suggested minimum price of 100 to 200 won, as reported by Korea JoongAng Daily. No start date was given in that report, so staff may ask whether your drink is for here or to go, and you may pay for a takeaway cup in future.

FAQ

Is it rude to stay in a Korean cafe for hours?

Not if you keep buying and stay considerate. Many people work for two to three hours on one drink, but owners complain about seat-hogging, so order again if you stay long and take your belongings if you leave for a while.

How much is a coffee in Korea?

Roughly 1,700 won for a hot Americano at a low-cost chain, and about 4,100 to 4,700 won at mainstream cafes. Specialty and themed cafes often charge more, so check the menu board.

Can I bring my laptop to a Korean cafe?

Usually yes, and many cafes have outlets, but rules vary. Starbucks Korea has banned desktops, printers, partitions and multi-plug power strips since August 7, 2025, and some independent cafes cap stays or cover outlets.

What is a study cafe in Korea?

A paid, quiet space for studying that is often unmanned and sold by the day or month, with monthly fees reported at 150,000 to 200,000 won. It is aimed at residents rather than tourists, and a regular cafe is the better choice for a coffee break.
