Korean Sunscreen: How to Choose and Where to Buy

By Dragon Kim · Updated November 28, 2026 · 7 min read

Quick answer

Check two ratings first: SPF, up to SPF50+, for UVB, and PA, from PA+ to PA++++, for UVA. Then pick a texture you will reapply, look for a water-resistance label if you will swim or sweat, and keep carry-on containers at 100 ml or less.

What should I know before I arrive?

Details checked November 28, 2026

In Korea, sunscreen is sold within a regulated category called functional cosmetics, so look for that mark as well as the ratings. The first of four steps from the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (MFDS), as summarised in a June 2026 government article (in Korean), is to use a product marked as a functional cosmetic (기능성화장품).

  • Worth knowing: A functional cosmetic mark is a regulatory category, not a medical treatment. Our Korean skincare routine guide shows where sunscreen sits in a daily routine.
  • A common mistake: Choosing by the SPF number alone and ignoring PA, texture and water resistance.
  • Local tip: Save the Korean words 자외선차단 (UV protection), 내수성 (water resistant) and 사용기한 (use-by date) to match them against a box.
  • Home rules differ: Filters and label terms are not the same in every country. If you need a product that meets your home rules, check those before you buy.

What do SPF and PA mean on the label?

SPF rates protection against UVB and PA rates protection against UVA; a higher number and more plus signs mean more protection. Read both, because one does not tell you the other.

  • SPF50+: Korea Medical Times (November 15, 2026, in Korean) explains that values of 50 and above are labelled SPF50+ and that PA has four levels.
  • Not a total block: The government article reports MFDS saying the real difference above SPF 50 is not large, and that the label can be misread as complete blocking.
  • Rule change ahead: MFDS revised its functional cosmetics review notice on November 10, 2026, with effect from November 11, 2027. The report above says it accepts new laboratory test methods for SPF and PA and adds evidence requirements for label claims. It is not in force during this winter or the 2027 summer.

Which rating should I pick for my day?

Match the rating to how long you will be outdoors: moderate for errands, higher for hiking, snow or the beach. The levels below come from older Korean government guidance published in June 2012, so treat them as a rough floor and not a current medical rule.

Walking around town, commutingSPF20, PA+ or higherA texture comfortable under makeup
Skiing or water sportsSPF30, PA++ or higherWater resistance label

Practical example: you plan a winter day at a ski resort and an evening in Seoul. Take a water-resistant tube of at least SPF30 and PA++ for the slopes, and carry it with you so you can top up at lunch. Our Korea winter packing guide covers the rest of the bag.

For comparison, the US Food and Drug Administration recommends broad-spectrum sunscreen of SPF 15 or higher, including on cloudy days. That is US guidance, and US labels do not use the PA system described here.

Which texture should I choose?

Choose the format you will use generously and reapply, then confirm its SPF, PA and water-resistance wording. Texture names are shop and brand wording, so the same word can feel different between products.

  • Cream or lotion: Easy to measure for the face and body. A sensible default for most visitors.
  • Gel, essence or serum type: Often sold as lighter on the skin. Test for stickiness and pilling under makeup.
  • Stick: Compact for topping up. It is harder to judge how much you have applied.
  • Spray or mist: For the face, spray into your hands first and then apply, which both the Korean article and the FDA advise. Avoid breathing it in.
  • Tinted or cushion: Combines colour with UV protection. A thin makeup layer may be far less than the tested amount.
  • Mineral formulas: If you prefer them, look for zinc oxide or titanium dioxide in the ingredient list.

Patch-test a new product on a small area first. If it stings or causes a rash, stop using it and speak to a pharmacist or doctor. We do not rank brands or recommend particular products.

Where can I buy sunscreen in Korea?

Health and beauty chains, brand cosmetics shops, department store counters and duty-free shops all sell it; chains make side-by-side comparison easy. Prices vary by size, brand and promotion, and we did not verify a typical price, so compare the price per millilitre on the shelf.

  1. Pick a store: For example, the Olive Young Myeongdong Town store is at 53 Myeongdong-gil, listed 10:00 to 22:30 daily and reached from Myeongdong Station Line 4, Exit 8. Hours can change.
  2. Or use another branch: VisitKorea lists the Olive Young Myeong-dong Station branch at 115 Toegye-ro, open 10:30 to 22:30.
  3. Test: Try the texture on your hand and check for a white cast in natural light.
  4. Read the box: Confirm SPF, PA, volume and use-by date.
  5. Ask for a tax refund: The Myeongdong Town page says purchases of 15,000 won or more qualify for an immediate refund with your passport. Other shops must be participating tax refund shops; see our tax refund guide.

The same store page says unopened regular products can be exchanged or refunded within 15 days with the receipt and payment method, with refunds only at the branch of purchase. For more on shopping the area, read the Myeongdong K-beauty street guide and the Olive Young guide.

How much should I apply and how often?

Use about 0.8 g on an adult face, apply 15 minutes before going out, and reapply often when sweating or in long sun. This follows the June 2026 government article on MFDS guidance.

  • Why 0.8 g: Ratings are tested at 2 mg per square centimetre. The article works out about 0.74 g for an average adult female face and 0.84 g for a male face.
  • Two-finger rule: It compares this to lines of product along your index and middle fingers. Treat that as a rough measure.
  • Thin layers: If a full amount feels heavy, spread one thin layer and add the same again.
  • Body: The FDA gives about one ounce (roughly 30 ml) to cover an average adult's body, and says to reapply at least every two hours.
  • Water: No sunscreen is waterproof, according to the FDA. Reapply after swimming or towelling.
  • Babies: Both the FDA and the 2012 Korean guidance advise against ordinary use on infants under six months. Ask a doctor.

Can I fly home with Korean sunscreen?

Yes: on international flights, carry containers of 100 ml or less in one 1 L transparent zip bag, and check larger ones. Incheon Airport's restricted items FAQ names sunscreen among gels.

  • Duty free: The restricted items page says duty-free liquids of any volume are allowed in a sealed duty-free liquid item bag, kept closed until you board the flight to your final destination.
  • Sprays: The same page limits non-flammable sprays in checked bags to 500 ml per item and 2 L in total. Ask your airline about flammable cans.
  • Connections: A transit airport or your airline may apply its own rules.
  • Heat and leaks: Close caps firmly and seal tubes in a bag inside checked luggage.

FAQ

What does PA++++ mean on Korean sunscreen?

PA is the rating for protection against UVA rays. Korea shows it in four levels, from PA+ to PA++++, and more plus signs mean more UVA protection. SPF, the number beside it, is the rating for UVB.

Is SPF50+ much better than SPF50?

Do not expect a big jump. Korea writes values of 50 and above as SPF50+, and the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety has cautioned that the difference above 50 is not large and does not mean complete blocking.

Can I take Korean sunscreen in my carry-on?

Yes, if each container holds 100 ml or less and fits in your one 1 L transparent zip bag on international flights. Incheon Airport lists sunscreen among gels. Larger containers go in checked baggage unless sealed duty-free purchases.

How much Korean sunscreen should I put on my face?

A June 2026 government article based on Ministry of Food and Drug Safety guidance puts it at about 0.8 grams for an adult face, similar to two finger-length lines. Reapply often when sweating or in long sun.
