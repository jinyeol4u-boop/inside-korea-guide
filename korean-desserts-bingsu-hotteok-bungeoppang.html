Korean Desserts: Bingsu, Hotteok and Bungeoppang Guide

By Dragon Kim · Updated November 11, 2026 · 7 min read

Quick answer

Bingsu is shaved ice, about 4,400 to 6,300 won at coffee chains and 9,500 won for classic patbingsu in Seoul (VisitKorea, July 2025). Hotteok, a filled sweet pancake, was listed at 2,500 won in Namdaemun, and bungeoppang costs about three for 2,000 won. The last two are winter street snacks.

What should I know before I order Korean desserts?

Details checked November 11, 2026

The three famous desserts follow the seasons: bingsu is the cold one, while hotteok and bungeoppang are hot street snacks that appear when the weather cools. Bingsu started out as a summer treat, but the Korea JoongAng Daily reports that franchises now serve it year-round, so you can find it in any month. The Korea Herald groups hotteok-style griddle snacks and fish-shaped pastries among winter street snacks.

  • Worth knowing: Prices below come from articles dated between 2023 and 2025, so treat them as a guide. Stall prices rise and fall with ingredient costs, and an official fixed price list does not exist.
  • A common mistake: Expecting a stall to be open at any hour. One Gwangjang Market bungeoppang shop was listed with limited hours of noon to 5 p.m. and closed Mondays and Fridays, per the Korea Times in December 2024.
  • Local tip: Carry some small bills and a card. Our cash or card guide explains what stalls usually accept, and our street food guide covers savory snacks.

What is bingsu and how much does it cost?

Bingsu is shaved ice topped with sweet ingredients; the classic version, patbingsu, uses sweet red beans, and prices run from about 4,000 won to well over 100,000 won. The Korea JoongAng Daily says the modern form is thought to have come from Japan during colonial rule (1910 to 1945), and that "pat" means red beans, which is why the old name was patbingsu. Later versions added fruit, condensed milk, grain powder and rice cake.

VisitKorea's bingsu page, last updated July 2025, lists these examples. Hours and prices come from that page and may have changed:

  • Dongbinggo, Ichon-dong, Yongsan-gu: patbingsu 9,500 won, open 10:30 to 22:00.
  • Patgodang Myeongdong main branch: patbingsu 14,000 won, open 10:00 to 22:00.
  • Busan: Namcheon Nokcha Patbingsu lists patbingsu at 4,000 won, and the Nampo-dong Patbingsu Alley lists 5,000 to 6,000 won.
  • Coffee chains: Ediya one-person bingsu 6,300 won, and Mega Coffee and Compose Coffee bingsu drinks at 4,400 to 4,500 won. The page advises calling ahead for hours.
  • Luxury hotels: The Shilla Seoul's apple mango bingsu is listed at 110,000 won. This is a special-occasion price, not a typical one.

What is hotteok and where can I find it?

Hotteok is a sweet pancake made from sticky dough, usually filled with sugar, and the Korea Times lists a japchae-filled version at 2,500 won in Namdaemun Market. According to that December 2024 guide, the vendor tears off dough, adds the filling, seals it, fries it on an oiled pan, flattens it and flips it until golden and crispy.

  • Fillings named: sugar, honey, brown sugar, grain seeds (especially popular in Busan), red bean paste, japchae, kimchi, cheese, ice cream, seafood and others.
  • Where it was listed: Namdaemun Market in Seoul, Insa-dong, Gukje Market in Busan and Jungang Market in Sokcho.

If you plan a market day, pair it with our Gwangjang Market guide or the Myeongdong street food guide.

What is bungeoppang and why is it hard to find?

Bungeoppang is a fish-shaped pastry, usually filled with red bean paste, and the Korea Times reports typical street prices of three for 2,000 won, though some downtown Seoul vendors charge 1,000 won each. The same guide lists Chonggakne Bungeoppang at Gwangjang Market with red bean and custard at 1,500 won and other fillings at 2,000 won, and a Seongsu-dong shop charging 4,500 won for a premium red bean and salted butter version.

Stalls are getting rarer. An Asia Economy report from November 2025 says Seoul street vendors fell from 6,079 in 2020 to 4,741, and that on one day only 3 of 15 bungeoppang stalls it checked in four Seoul districts were operating. It also says a citizen-made "Bungeoppang Map" app exists, though closures make it hard to keep current. A convenience-store option exists as well: the Korea Herald noted that GS25 launched a 1,200-won version in September 2023.

How do the three desserts compare?

Choose bingsu for a cool, shareable dessert, and hotteok or bungeoppang for a cheap, hot snack to eat on the street.

What other sweet snacks should I look for?

Look for kukhwappang, sipwonppang and gyeranppang, which share the same stalls and cold-weather season. The Korea Herald lists kukhwappang (chrysanthemum-shaped, about 2,000 won for three or four in 2023) and sipwonppang, a coin-shaped waffle usually 3,000 to 4,000 won with a cheese or custard filling. The Korea Times adds gyeranppang, a muffin-style bread with a whole egg, found near Sillim Station and in Myeong-dong. For savory street snacks, see our street food guide.

FAQ

When is the best season for bingsu, hotteok and bungeoppang?

Bingsu began as a summer treat, but a Korea JoongAng Daily feature says franchises now serve it all year. Hotteok and bungeoppang are usually described as winter street snacks, so late autumn to winter is when most stalls are out.

How much does bingsu cost in Korea?

It ranges widely. VisitKorea's July 2025 listing shows a classic patbingsu from 4,000 won in Busan and 9,500 won in Seoul, 4,400 to 6,300 won at coffee chains, and 110,000 won or more for hotel apple mango bingsu. Check the menu board, since prices change.

Where can I find hotteok in Seoul?

The Korea Times named Namdaemun Market, where one stall sells japchae-filled hotteok for 2,500 won, and also listed Insa-dong. Busan's Gukje Market is another place it named. Stalls come and go, so confirm on the spot.

Why is it hard to find bungeoppang stalls?

A November 2025 Asia Economy report said street vendors in Seoul fell from 6,079 in 2020 to 4,741, and that only 3 of 15 bungeoppang stalls it checked in four districts were open on one day. Cafes and convenience stores sell versions too.

Are these desserts suitable for vegetarians?

Many are, because the classic fillings are red bean or sugar, but some hotteok fillings listed by the Korea Times include seafood and galbi, so ask what is inside. Individual recipes and doughs vary, so check ingredients if you have dietary needs.
